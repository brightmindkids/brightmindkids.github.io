Is This a Plum? wins Children's Book of the Year: why peek-through books are so good for little readers

This week, UK bookshop chain Waterstones announced its Books of the Year for 2026, and the children's prize went to a picture book: Is This a Plum? by Dan and Finn Ojari, a father-and-son duo, published by Puffin. The winners were chosen by Waterstones booksellers from a shortlist of 20 titles. The overall Book of the Year went to an adult nonfiction title, so it is lovely to see a book for the youngest readers standing right beside it.

It is not the book's only honour this season. Earlier in November, Barnes & Noble in the United States named it its Children's Gift Book of the Year, describing it as an interactive peek-through book with clever wordplay.

What kind of book is it?

Is This a Plum? is a cut-out picture book built around visual surprises and cheeky humour. Holes in the pages let children see a small part of the next picture, so they guess what it might be, then turn the page to find out. Bea Carvalho, Waterstones' head of books, called it "a simple concept, exquisitely executed to hilarious effect". Booksellers also reported that children responded with real excitement during in-store story times.

Why peek-through books work so well

Books with holes, flaps and surprises turn reading into a game. For children aged 3 to 8, that matters. They are invited to look closely, make a guess, say it out loud and then check it. That little loop of predicting and discovering is a skill good readers use all the time, long before they can decode words on their own. These books are also made for reading aloud: the pause before the page turn gives everyone a moment of suspense and lots of laughter.

How to read a peek-through book together

  • Slow down before each page turn. Ask, "What do you think it is?" and accept every guess, even silly ones.
  • Ask why. "What made you think that?" helps your child notice colours, shapes and details.
  • Let your child turn the page for the big reveal. Being in charge of the surprise is half the fun.
  • Read it again and swap roles: your child asks the questions and you do the guessing.
  • For older children, point out rhymes and word jokes, and invite them to invent a new page of their own.

You do not need this exact title to enjoy the idea. Your library is likely to have plenty of flap, cut-out and guessing books, and many of them stand up to dozens of rereads.

Try this tonight

Tonight, cut a small hole in a sheet of paper, lay it over a picture in any book, and let your child guess what is hiding underneath before you lift it.
