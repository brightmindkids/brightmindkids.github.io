Afraid of the dark? Gentle ways to help little ones with bedtime fears

If your little one suddenly needs the hall light on, checks under the bed or calls you back three times after lights out, you are far from alone. Paediatric sleep teams describe night-time fears as a normal part of growing up: most children have them at some point, and fear of the dark is one of the most common.

Children's Hospital Colorado notes that nearly three in four children aged 4 to 12 report night-time fears. They tend to appear as children's imaginations grow and they begin to understand that things can go wrong, while young children still find it hard to separate what is real from what is imagined.

What helps

  • Talk about the fear in daylight, when everyone is calm, rather than at the tense moment of lights out.
  • Take the fear seriously. Don't tease, even gently, and don't try to argue it away.
  • Keep the bedtime routine predictable: bath, pyjamas, story, cuddle, lights out, in the same order every night.
  • Offer small tools for control: a soft night light, a favourite cuddly toy, or a torch they can switch on themselves.
  • Practise brave thoughts together, such as 'The dark is just my room with the lights off.'
  • Read stories about characters who feel scared and discover they are safe after all.
  • Show quiet confidence that your child can handle it. Children borrow our calm.

Most children grow out of these fears over a few weeks or months. If fears are severe, last a long time or affect daytime life, it is worth talking to your child's doctor.

Try this tonight

Before bed tonight, walk around the darkened room together with a torch and name everything you find: the chair, the books, the teddy. Familiar shapes turn the dark back into home.
