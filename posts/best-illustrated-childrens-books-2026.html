The 10 Best Illustrated Children's Books of 2026, picked by the NYT and New York Public Library

Every year, The New York Times and the New York Public Library name ten picture books whose art stands out from everything else published that year. The award has been running for more than seven decades, and the 2026 list was chosen by a children's librarian, Kelly-Ann Smith, and two picture-book creators, Julie Fogliano and Edel Rodriguez.

It is a list judged on illustration, so it is a great place to look if you want books your child will pore over again and again, even before they can read the words.

The 2026 winners

  • While We're Here by Anne Wynter, illustrated by Micha Archer: cut-paper collage that keeps pace with the rhythm of the text.
  • Duel by Shawn Harris: playful, inkblot-style pictures painted in tempera and acrylic.
  • Ahab and the White Whale by Manuel Marsol (translated by Chloe Garcia Roberts): thick, textured paintings that show off the fun of paint itself.
  • Mariposa's Journey by Belen Medina, illustrated by Juana Martinez-Neal: a girl looking for a safe new home, told alongside the monarch butterfly migration.
  • Night Ride by Christopher Myers: a wordless night-time bike ride through New York, drawn like stained glass.
  • Bored by Felicita Sala: a funny, very familiar look at what boredom feels like.
  • Goldfinches by Mary Oliver, illustrated by Melissa Sweet: mixed-media art that feels like a nature walk and a bird-watcher's notebook.
  • Kiki & Me by Leo Timmers (translated by David Colmer): a horse tells the story of a friendship, in big painted spreads and comic panels.
  • Between by Anna Walker: soft, glowing pictures in just two colours, made with spray paint and pencil.
  • The Heart of Our Home by Janelle Washington: layered cut-paper scenes of family meals and everyday rituals.

How to use a list like this at home

  • Pick one or two titles that match something your child already loves: bikes, birds, horses, family.
  • Ask your local library to reserve them. Award lists are popular, so a hold saves a trip.
  • Try a 'picture walk' first: turn the pages together and talk only about the pictures before you read the words.
  • Wordless books like Night Ride are perfect for pre-readers. Let your child tell you the story.
Try this tonight

Choose one illustration tonight and ask your child: 'What do you think happens just after this picture?' It builds imagination, vocabulary and storytelling in one question.
