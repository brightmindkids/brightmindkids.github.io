Reading together is back: why schools are turning to shared reading

Teachers in many countries report the same worry: fewer children choose to read for fun, and it is getting harder to keep them engaged with books at school and at home. A new report from Education Week in October 2026 looked at what schools are doing about it, and one idea stands out: reading together.

In one sixth-grade classroom in Georgia, featured in the report, students now do most of their reading as a group, in class. The aims are simple: build fluency, create a shared buzz around the book, and make sure every child actually reaches the last page. The report's wider message is that a mix of adults modelling reading, teacher guidance, small rewards and family involvement works better than any single trick.

Schools are also reaching out to families. The report describes community reading nights where parents and young children read side by side, and school librarians who work with teachers to build a reading culture from the very first years of school.

This fits with UK findings from the National Literacy Trust in 2026: reading enjoyment ticked up for the first time in five years, but remains low, and children are much more likely to read regularly when reading is linked to their interests and supported by the people around them.

Bring shared reading home

  • Pick one 'family book' and read a chapter or a few pages together every evening.
  • Take turns: you read a page, your child reads a line or a speech bubble.
  • Stop at an exciting moment and guess what happens next.
  • Let siblings join in: older children can read to younger ones.
  • Celebrate finishing: a special snack, a drawing of the ending, or choosing the next book.
Try this tonight

Shared reading does not have to be long. Ten minutes of reading the same story together, at the same time each day, does more for the habit than one long session at the weekend.
