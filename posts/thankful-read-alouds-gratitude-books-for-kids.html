Thankful read-alouds: gratitude picture books and a simple story-time ritual

Thanksgiving falls on Thursday in the United States, and library displays and bookshop tables are full of turkeys, harvest tables and thank-you stories. Wherever you live, this is a lovely week to borrow the idea behind the holiday: noticing the good things in our lives and saying so out loud. Picture books make that easy, because the characters do the noticing first.

Gratitude books for the littlest listeners

  • Duck, Duck, Gobble! by J.D. Forester, illustrated by Lydia Nichols: a board book in which a duck and other farm animals turn a holiday table upside down. School Library Journal suggests it as a lap-sit read for toddlers.
  • Construction Site: A Thankful Night by Sherri Duskey Rinker, illustrated by Helen Morgan: a rhyming lift-the-flap book where the trucks say thank you for their work.
  • Thanksgiving Friends by Tammi Salzano, illustrated by Seb Braun: a touch-and-feel introduction to the holiday and to friendship.

Picture books for ages 4 to 8

  • It's Almost Time for... Thanksgiving! by Maisha Oso, illustrated by Tom Knight: a new 2026 title in which a family gets ready with a menu, a parade and loved ones, while a rhyme keeps landing on the wrong holiday. Kirkus praised its bouncy verse and busy, detail-filled pictures.
  • The Crayons Give Thanks by Drew Daywalt, illustrated by Oliver Jeffers: the famous box of crayons from The Day the Crayons Quit say what they are grateful for.
  • At Our Table by Patrick Hulse, illustrated by Madelyn Goodnight: a look at what the holiday means to many different families.
  • Our Table by Peter H. Reynolds: Violet looks for ways to bring her busy family back together around the table, a gentle story about connection.

Turn any story into a thankful moment

You do not need a special book to talk about gratitude. Scholastic's parent guide suggests open-ended questions while you read, and they work with almost any story. Try one or two per book rather than all at once:

  • What do you think this character is thankful for?
  • Who was kind in this story? How did it make the others feel?
  • If you were in the book, who would you want to thank, and why?
  • What is one way our family could show thanks this week?

Younger children may answer with a single word, such as a toy, a pet or pancakes, and that is perfect. Older children can draw or dictate a short thank-you note to someone from their own life, inspired by the characters.

Why this helps

Talking about a character's feelings gives children words for their own. Linking a story to real people, like a grandparent, a teacher or a friend, also builds comprehension, because kids practise connecting what they read to their world.

Try this tonight

Tonight, put a small jar next to the bedtime books. After the story, each person names one thing they were thankful for today, and a grown-up writes it on a slip of paper for the jar. Read them all together at the end of the month.
