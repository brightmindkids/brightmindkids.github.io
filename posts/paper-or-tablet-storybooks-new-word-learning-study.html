Paper book or tablet? What a new study says about learning words at story time

Many families now own both shelves of picture books and a tablet loaded with e-books, and parents often wonder whether one is better for learning. A study from Lancaster University in the UK, published in the Journal of Experimental Child Psychology and reported this week, offers a reassuring answer, with a useful twist.

What the researchers did

Laura Diprossimo and Kate Cain worked with 99 children aged 4 and 5 and their caregivers. Each pair shared two storybooks: one on paper and one on an iPad. The digital books were deliberately plain, with no narration, animations or games, so the only real difference was the screen itself. Afterwards, children were tested on new words from the stories and on how well they understood them.

What they found

  • On average, children learned new words about equally well from paper and from the tablet.
  • Children who already had a bigger vocabulary picked up more new words in both formats.
  • Children with stronger attention and self-control skills understood the story slightly better on the tablet, while children with weaker skills in those areas did better with paper.
  • Caregivers pointed, explained and asked fewer questions when reading from the tablet.

That last finding may matter most at home. The extra pointing, gesturing and chatting adults do around a story is a big part of how children learn new words, and it seems to fade a little when a screen is involved.

Keep it in perspective

The study tested only simple e-books, not the interactive apps with sounds and games that many children use. Word learning was checked just a few minutes after reading, and most families taking part had university-educated parents. So this is one helpful piece of evidence, not the final word.

What this means for your family

  • Both formats can work. A tablet book on a long car journey is not a lost reading moment.
  • Whatever the format, the conversation is the magic. Pause to point at pictures and explain unusual words.
  • If your child is easily distracted, paper books may help them follow the story.
  • Choose plain e-books over ones packed with games and pop-ups when the goal is reading together.
Try this tonight

Tonight, pick one new word from the bedtime story, such as 'enormous' or 'shiver'. Point to it in the picture, act it out together, then try to use it again at breakfast tomorrow.
